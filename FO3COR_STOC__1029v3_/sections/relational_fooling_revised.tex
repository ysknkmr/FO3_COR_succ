\subsection{Limits of the relational fooling-set argument}\label{sec:natural-approaches}
% \subsection{Natural approaches that fail}\label{sec:natural-approaches}
A natural approach is to extend
the earlier exponential lower bound for \defn{$\Sigma_1\CoR$},
even with shared subterms~\citep[Theorems~6.1 and~6.12]{nakamura2022}.
Here $\Sigma_1\CoR$ allows complement only on
input relations and $\id$ (after pushing it to the leaves):
$\overline R;\one$ is permitted, but $\overline{R;\one}$ is not.
Although the original proof in~\citep{nakamura2022} uses formula-size
games in the style of Adler and Immerman~\citep{adler-immerman2003},
it can be viewed as a relational fooling-set argument.
We first recall the fooling-set argument.

The \defn{fooling-set argument}~\citep{yao1979,lipton-sedgewick1981,kushilevitz-nisan1997,rao-yehudayoff2020}
is a standard technique for proving lower bounds in communication complexity.
For a Boolean function $f\colon A\times B\to\{0,1\}$, a
\defn{fooling set} is a set $F\subseteq f^{-1}(1)$ such that any two
distinct $(a,b),(a',b')\in F$ have a crossed input $(a,b')$ or
$(a',b)$ of value $0$. A \defn{1\nobreakdash-rectangle} is a product
$A'\times B'\subseteq f^{-1}(1)$. Since a rectangle containing two
points also contains their crossings, each 1\nobreakdash-rectangle
covers at most one point of $F$, so at least $|F|$ are needed.

Consider Nakamura's query of size $O(k)$,
$\varphi_k(x,y)\coloneqq\exists z\,\bigwedge_{i=1}^k
(R_i(x,z)\lor R_i(z,y))$~\cite{nakamura2022}.
Let $I,J\subseteq[k]$ record the relation labels on the two witness
edges $(x,z)$ and $(z,y)$. The witness condition is precisely
$f_k(I,J)=1\iff I\cup J=[k]$, with the fooling set
$F_k=\{(I,I^c):\varnothing\subsetneq I\subsetneq[k]\}$ of size
$2^k-2$ for $k\ge2$. Each point $(I,I^c)$ is realized in
\citep[\S5]{nakamura2022} by a positive pointed structure
$(\mathcal S_I;u_I,v_I)$ with a witness path
$u_I\xrightarrow{\,I\,}w_I\xrightarrow{\,I^c\,}v_I$.
%
There is a subtlety: these paths belong to different structures, so
we cannot directly cross $(u_I,v_I)$ and $(u_J,v_J)$. The construction
provides a common negative pointed structure $(\mathcal N;u^*,v^*)$,
with
$\mathcal S_I\models\varphi_k(u_I,v_I)$ for every $I$ but
$\mathcal N\not\models\varphi_k(u^*,v^*)$.
Whenever $f_k(I,J^c)=0$, the left witness half $(u_I,w_I)$ and the
right half $(w_J,v_J)$ embed into $\mathcal N$ at $(u^*,c_{I,J})$ and
$(c_{I,J},v^*)$, respectively, using a vertex $c_{I, J}$.

\smallskip
\customparagB{How can we cross witnesses from different structures?}
The key is \defn{preservation under embeddings}: every $\Sigma_1\CoR$
term $V$ and embedding $\iota\colon\mathcal A\hookrightarrow\mathcal B$
satisfy
\[
\tag{$\diamond$}\label{eq:mono}
\iota\bigl(\sem V{\mathcal A}\bigr)\subseteq\sem V{\mathcal B},
\quad\text{or equivalently,}\quad
\mathcal A\models V(u,v)\ \Longrightarrow\
\mathcal B\models V(\iota(u),\iota(v)).
\]
%Thus, an accepted witness half remains accepted after embedding; this is exactly what makes relational crossing possible.

Now let $T$ be a $\Sigma_1\CoR$ term equivalent to $\varphi_k$.
A short structural induction on $T$ (after pushing converses to the
leaves, and using the embeddings to exclude endpoint witnesses)
shows that, for each $I$, some composition subterm $X;Y$  of $T$ satisfies
(1) $\mathcal S_I\models X(u_I,w_I)\land Y(w_I,v_I)$;
and
(2) $\mathcal N\not\models (X;Y)(u^*,v^*)$.
%
Suppose $\mathcal S_J\models X(u_J,w_J)\land Y(w_J,v_J)$ also holds for $I \neq J$.
Since $(I,I^c), (J,J^c) \in F_k$, at least one of their crossed inputs has value $0$. Suppose $f_k(I,J^c)=0$; the other case is symmetric. The crossing embeddings and $(\diamond)$ then give
$\mathcal N\models (X;Y)(u^*,v^*)$
because
$
\mathcal N\models X(u^*,c_{I,J})\land Y(c_{I,J},v^*)
$.
This contradicts $(2)$.
\if0
Then, since we have $\mathcal{S}_I \models (X; Y)(u_I, v_I)$ and $\mathcal{S}_J \models (X; Y)(u_J, v_J)$,
we can cross them as $(u_I, v_J)$ or $(u_J, v_I)$ in $\mathcal{N}$ as $\mathcal N\models (X;Y)(u^*,v^*)$ by applying~\eqref{eq:mono} so that
$\mathcal N\models X(u^*,c_{I,J})\land Y(c_{I,J},v^*)$.
However, it contradicts to $(2)$.
\fi

Thus, \textbf{such composition $X; Y$ plays the role of a 1-rectangle}, accounting for at most one fooling-set point.
The argument extends to shared subterms~\citep[\S6.3]{nakamura2022}.

\smallskip
\customparagB{Why complement defeats the argument.}
The crossing argument relies crucially on $(\diamond)$, 
which fails under unrestricted complement.
Indeed, $V := \overline{R;\one}$ violates $(\diamond)$.
$V(x, y)$ means $\neg\exists z. R(x,z)$ in \FOthree.
Let $\mathcal A$ have no $R$-successor of $u$, and obtain an extension $\mathcal B$ by adding a fresh $R$-successor. Then,
$\mathcal A\models V(u,v)$
and
$\mathcal B\not\models V(u,v)$.

Furthermore, the above $2^k-2$ positive instances $(\mathcal S_I; u_I, v_I)$ and the negative instance $(\mathcal N; u^*, v^*)$ can be distinguished by a single full-\CoR\ term with only two compositions.
Writing $E:=\bigcup_i(R_i\cup R_i^\smile)$,
consider $U:=\neg\bigl((E\cap(E;\neg\id));\one\bigr)$
where $\neg T$ means $\overline{T}$.
$U(x, y)$ means $\neg[\exists y\exists z.(E(x,y)\land E(x,z)\land y\ne z)]$ in \FOthree.
This holds in every $\mathcal S_I$ but fails in $\mathcal N$, where $u^*$ has at least two neighbors. Although $U$ does not define $\varphi_k$, the old test instances themselves cannot certify an exponential lower bound for full \CoR.
